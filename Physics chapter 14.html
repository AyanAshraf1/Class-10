1. Reflection of Light & Laws of Reflection

Reflection is the phenomenon where incident light rays strike a boundary interface and bounce back into the same medium from which they originated.

Laws of Reflection:

  1. First Law: The angle of incidence ($\theta_i$) is strictly equal to the angle of reflection ($\theta_r$). $$\theta_i = \theta_r$$
  2. Second Law: The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie within the exact same geometric plane.

2. Regular (Specular) vs. Diffused (Irregular) Reflection

Depending on the microscopic surface topography, reflection is categorized into two distinct behaviors:

Property Regular / Specular Reflection Diffused / Irregular Reflection
Surface Topography Smooth, polished surface (e.g., plane mirror, calm water). Rough, uneven, or unpolished surface (e.g., paper, wall, wood).
Reflected Ray Direction Parallel incident rays remain parallel after reflection. Parallel incident rays scatter in random, multiple directions.
Image Formation Forms crisp, distinct images. No image formed; illuminates the surrounding environment.
Validity of Laws Laws of reflection hold true across the entire macro surface. Laws hold true at every microscopic point, but microscopic surface normals vary randomly.
Current Mode: Regular (Specular) Surface

3 & 4. Refraction of Light, Speed, & Snell's Law

Refraction is the bending of light at an interface when passing obliquely from one transparent medium to another due to a change in its phase velocity.

Snell's Law:
$$\frac{\sin(\theta_1)}{\sin(\theta_2)} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$ Absolute Refractive Index ($n$):
$$n = \frac{c}{v}$$

5. Practical Experiment: Refraction Through Glass Slab

When light passes through a parallel-sided rectangular glass slab, it undergoes refraction twice. The emergent ray is laterally shifted (displaced) relative to the original incident path, but remains parallel to it ($\theta_i = \theta_e$).

Lateral Displacement Formula ($d$):
$$d = \frac{t \cdot \sin(\theta_i - \theta_r)}{\cos(\theta_r)}$$

6 & 7. Total Internal Reflection (TIR) & Optical Fibers

When light travels from an optically denser medium to a rarer medium, if the incident angle exceeds the Critical Angle ($\theta_c$), the ray cannot refract and undergoes 100% internal reflection.

Critical Angle Formula:
$$\sin(\theta_c) = \frac{n_{\text{rarer}}}{n_{\text{denser}}} \implies \theta_c = \arcsin\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)$$
Critical Angle ($\theta_c$): --°

Optical Fiber Transmission Architecture

An optical fiber consists of a high-index core ($n_1$) encased in a lower-index cladding ($n_2$). Light launched into the core hits the cladding boundary at $\theta > \theta_c$, propagating indefinitely via repeated TIR with near-zero signal loss.

10 & 11. Lens Formulas, Magnifying Glass, and Image Types

Core Lens Formulae

Lens Equation:

$$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u}$$

Linear Magnification:

$$m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{v}{u}$$

Lens Power:

$$P = \frac{1}{f\text{ (meters)}} \quad [\text{Diopters, D}]$$

Real vs. Virtual Image Comparison

  • Real Images: Formed by actual intersecting rays. Can be projected onto a physical screen. Always inverted. $m < 0$.
  • Virtual Images: Formed where rays only appear to originate. Cannot be projected onto a screen. Always erect. $m > 0$.

Simple Magnifying Glass Principle

A simple magnifier consists of a single converging (convex) lens. When an object is placed within its focal length ($u < f$), it produces an erect, magnified, virtual image on the same side as the object, allowing the human eye to resolve fine detail.

12. Visible Spectrum & Human Vision Defects

The Visible Light Spectrum

White light passing through a dispersive prism splits into its constituent colors (VIBGYOR: Violet $\approx 400\text{ nm}$ to Red $\approx 700\text{ nm}$) due to wavelength-dependent refraction ($n \propto 1/\lambda$). Violet bends the most; Red bends the least.

Human Vision Defects and Corrective Lenses

Myopia (Short-Sightedness)

Issue: Can see nearby objects clearly; distant objects are blurry.

Cause: Eyeball is too long or lens curvature is excessive. Light focuses in front of the retina.

Correction: Concave (Diverging) Lens.

Hypermetropia (Long-Sightedness)

Issue: Can see distant objects clearly; nearby objects are blurry.

Cause: Eyeball is too short or focal length is too long. Light focuses behind the retina.

Correction: Convex (Converging) Lens.

Status: Normal Healthy Vision (Focus directly on Retina)
