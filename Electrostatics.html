1. Static Charge & Charge Quantization

Electric charge ($q$) is an intrinsic fundamental property of subatomic particles that governs their electromagnetic interactions. Electrostatics studies charges at rest, where electrostatic forces dominate over magnetic effects.

Quantization of Charge:
$$q = \pm n e$$ Where $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ (an integer) and fundamental charge $e \approx 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$.

Coulomb's Law (Electrostatic Force):

$$\mathbf{F} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}$$ Where $\varepsilon_0 \approx 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\text{ F/m}$ is the permittivity of free space.

2. Law of Conservation of Charge

In any isolated thermodynamic system, the net algebraic sum of electric charges remains constant over time. Charge can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred between bodies or created in equal and opposite (+/-) particle-antiparticle pairs.

$$\sum q_{\text{initial}} = \sum q_{\text{final}}$$

High-Level Examples:

  • Triboelectric Transfer: Glass rubbed with silk leaves silk with $-q$ and glass with $+q$. Net system charge remains zero.
  • Pair Production: High-energy photon converts to an electron-positron pair: $\gamma \rightarrow e^- + e^+$. Net charge: $0 = (-1) + (+1)$.
  • Radioactive Beta Decay: Neutron decays into a proton, electron, and antineutrino: $n^0 \rightarrow p^+ + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$. Net charge remains $0$.

3. Conductors vs. Insulators (Microscopic Electronic Structure)

Rather than simple superficial definitions, the distinction between conductors and insulators stems from atomic lattice arrangement, band theory, and electron mobility.

Mode: Metallic Conductor (Free Delocalized Electron Sea)
Parameter Conductors (e.g., Metals, Graphite) Insulators (e.g., Glass, Ebonite, Quartz)
Electronic Band Structure Overlapping Valence & Conduction Bands ($E_g \approx 0\text{ eV}$). Large Forbidden Energy Band Gap ($E_g > 5\text{ eV}$).
Delocalized Electrons Abundant (~$10^{28}\text{ m}^{-3}$). Valence electrons leave parent atoms to form a "mobile electron sea". Virtually Zero. Tightly bound covalent or ionic valence electrons localized around specific nuclei.
Response to External Field ($\mathbf{E}$) Free electrons drift rapidly opposite to $\mathbf{E}$ until internal field cancels external field ($\mathbf{E}_{\text{inside}} = 0$). No charge drift. Molecules undergo **dielectric polarization** (electric dipole realignment).
Charge Distribution Excess charge resides strictly on the outer surface due to mutual repulsion. Excess charge remains localized at the exact region where placed.

4. Methods of Charging

1. Friction (Triboelectric)

Mechanical rubbing forces intimate atomic contact between two different insulators. Electrons transfer from the material with lower work function to the one with higher electron affinity.

2. Conduction (Direct Contact)

A charged conductor contacts a neutral conductor. Delocalized electrons flow due to potential gradient until both reach electrostatic potential equilibrium.

3. Electrostatic Induction (Contactless Charging)

A charged rod polarizes a neutral conductor without physical contact. Grounding the far end removes like charge, leaving the conductor oppositely charged.

5. Methods of Discharging Insulators and Conductors

Discharging Conductors

  • Grounding (Earthing): Connecting conductor to Earth via a low-resistance path. Earth acts as an infinite charge sink/source ($V_{\text{earth}} \equiv 0$). Electrons flow to/from Earth to neutralize the body.
  • Direct Mechanical Contact: Touching conductor with an earthed object (like a human hand) conducts away free delocalized electrons.

Discharging Insulators

Because electrons cannot flow through insulators, localized surface charges require distinct discharge mechanisms:

  • Gaseous Ionization (Action of Points / Corona Discharge): Sharp points create extremely high local fields ($\mathbf{E} \propto 1/r$). Surrounding air breaks down ($E > 3 \times 10^6\text{ V/m}$), forming ionized air ions that neutralize localized insulator charge.
  • Flame / Ultraviolet Exposure: Heat or energetic UV photons ionize surrounding air molecules, producing free ions that adsorb onto insulator surfaces to cancel charge.
  • Humidity & Surface Moisture Conduction: Atmospheric water molecules condense, forming a thin microscopic conductive layer on the insulator that bleeds charge away.

7. Electric Field ($\mathbf{E}$) & Field Lines

An **Electric Field** is a vector field describing the electrostatic force exerted on a unit positive test charge placed at any point in space.

$$\mathbf{E} = \frac{\mathbf{F}}{q_0} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \quad [\text{Unit: N/C or V/m}]$$

Fundamental Field Line Rules:

  1. Start on positive charges and end on negative charges (or extend to infinity).
  2. The tangent to a field line at any point gives the direction of the electric field vector $\mathbf{E}$.
  3. Field lines **never intersect** (if they did, $\mathbf{E}$ would have two directions at one point).
  4. Line density is proportional to electric field magnitude ($\vert{}\mathbf{E}\vert{}$).
  5. Field lines hit conductive surfaces at strictly perpendicular ($90^\circ$) angles.

8. Industrial Applications of Electrostatics

1. Electrostatic Precipitator (ESP)

Industrial smokestack pollution control. High voltage wires ($-50\text{ kV}$) produce a corona discharge, ionizing flue gas. Dust particles pick up negative charge and are pulled by strong electric fields onto grounded collector plates.

2. Xerography (Photocopying)

Uses a photoconductive selenium drum charged electrostatically in the dark. Light reflected from a document discharges illuminated drum areas, leaving a charged image pattern. Positively charged black toner powder sticks to dark drum areas and transfers onto heated paper.
